Research

Non-state involvement in state security

My work examines why and how states delegate security functions to non-state actors—pro-government militias, peacekeepers, and private firms—and what that delegation means for accountability, information, and control.

PGMs for Penetrative Political Control: The Underlying Logic of State–PGM Collaborations

Devin P. Brown

States collaborate with pro-government militias (PGMs) for reasons that extend well beyond delivering violence—only around 60% of PGMs are reported to have committed violence at all. This paper argues states also rely on militias for local information and political control, using them to reward supporters and identify and punish resistance. The argument is supported with statistical modeling and a case study of Turkey’s Village Guard system.

Pro-government Militias, Plausible Deniability, and Public Opinion: Testing the Microfoundations

Devin P. Brown

Does delegating violence to militias let governments plausibly deny responsibility? Using survey experiments, this paper tests whether the formal and operational separation between a government and an affiliated militia shapes how observers assign blame and how that, in turn, affects public support for the government.

Strongmen in Need of Help: Personalist Regimes, Small Militaries, and Peacekeeping Acceptance

Devin P. Brown and Hyeran Jo

Why do personalist dictatorships—regimes especially wary of outside interference—invite UN peacekeeping operations onto their territory? Using regime data from 1990–2010, this paper weighs the sovereignty costs of accepting peacekeepers against the security benefits they provide to leaders whose own militaries are too small or unreliable to guarantee their hold on power.

Work in progress

Weapons and World Views: Border Security Expos and U.S. Border Security Policy

Devin P. Brown and Beth Simmons

This project explores how private defense firms and the trade shows they attend—most notably the Border Security Expo—shape U.S. border security policy. Drawing on original fieldwork at the Expo, we link firm participation to subsequent federal contracting patterns to trace the private origins and diffusion of border policy. Data collection is planned through Winter 2026, with submission targeted for Spring 2027.
